I am James McInerney, Professor of Evolutionary Biology and Head of the Department of Evolution, Ecology and Behaviour at the University of Liverpool. My group works out how genomes are put together.

2017

The variation is adaptation, not noise.

Theory says a species should not carry this much difference in gene content. With Alan McNally and Mary O'Connell, I argued that it can: in vast populations that keep moving into new niches, selection holds on to whichever genes pay their way locally.

Why prokaryotes have pangenomes, Nature Microbiology

2024

So the contents of a genome can be predicted.

Alan Beavan and Maria Rosa Domingo-Sananes trained a model to say whether a gene is present in an E. coli genome, given only the other genes there. For a substantial set of genes it works, across strains with very different histories.

Contingency, repeatability, and predictability in the evolution of a prokaryotic pangenome, PNAS

2014

The same process built complex cells.

Every animal, plant and fungus is made of cells that began as a merger of an archaeon and a bacterium. Mary O'Connell, Davide Pisani and I brought together the evidence from genes, fossils, energetics and cell biology, and found that it all points the same way.

The hybrid nature of the Eukaryota and a consilient view of life on Earth, Nature Reviews Microbiology

Now

And a genome can be read like a sentence.

If genes follow rules about their neighbours, a genome has grammar. panGPT is a language model we train on bacterial genomes, with genes as its words, on Isambard-AI, the UK's national AI supercomputer.

About

I studied microbiology at University College Galway and stayed for a PhD on archaea in the north-eastern Atlantic. Since then I have worked at the Natural History Museum in London, at Maynooth University for sixteen years, and at Manchester, Nottingham and Liverpool. All my papers are on ORCID.
